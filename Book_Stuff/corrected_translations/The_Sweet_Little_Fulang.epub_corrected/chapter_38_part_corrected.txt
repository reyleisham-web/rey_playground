Chapter 38


In the chestnut grove, deep and untouched by people, lay a carpet of hairy chestnuts scattered on the ground. Gray-brown squirrels climbed down from the trees to forage, stuffing chestnuts into their mouths and hauling them back to their nests to store for the winter. Not only squirrels, mice and voles hid chestnuts and hazelnuts in their holes as well.

There were plenty of such treasures in the deep forest, and people had to hurry to pick them up while they were still on the ground, lest they break or be carried off by the forest dwellers. It was Lu Gu’s first time coming to this chestnut grove, and he saw a layer of hairy chestnuts covering the earth—far more than at the previous mountain. He set down the bamboo basket and began gathering them, picking out the heavier ones; the chestnuts inside were watery and sweet. The smaller ones didn’t matter if they were light—the basket would fill eventually. Occasionally, ripe chestnuts fell from the trees and clattered onto the ground.

They had brought along the dogs, and the squirrels and voles were vigilant, occasionally poking their heads out from trees or holes. They watched the two-legged intruders with wary eyes.

Shen Xuanqing spotted a big-tailed squirrel high in a tree, but he didn’t shoot at it. He bent down to pick chestnuts, yet two small dogs sniffed around in the woods, digging at the ground. In one hole, a mouse’s scream could be heard, but it couldn’t escape the dogs’ pursuit, and even the hazelnuts and chestnuts stored in the hole were dug out. Hearing the disturbance, Shen Xuanqing saw a fat field mouse in the dogs’ mouths, not the slender, irritating kind; thus he ceased worrying about the little dogs and planned to roast it for them later. As for the contents of the field mouse’s hole, no one claimed them—there were enough chestnuts on the ground.

The animals’ caches, like the ones in the mouse holes, were usually dug up when there was nothing to eat, or when chestnuts couldn’t be found in the mountains; children and dogs alike would dig the holes. Anyway, the nuts had to be shelled, so they carried them back, washed them, and found the washing kept them clean. Children were greedy and playful, and digging holes was fun. For the adults, the mountain fauna—squirrels and voles—had to survive the winter; they wouldn’t starve because it wasn’t a famine year and food was plentiful, so letting them live gave them a way to survive too.

The five of them carried five bamboo baskets and two large sacks. They filled them up after a long spell of gathering in the grove. In such a vast chestnut grove, there were still some chestnuts left clinging to the trees. They were the only ones here to pick them, and one trip was enough for this year; they didn’t have to worry about whether the squirrels would have enough to eat.

Except for the one on Shen Yan’s back, the bamboo baskets were all large; they were heavy when full, so they had to take turns carrying the sacks. Shen Xuanqing carried the bamboo baskets on his back and shouldered the sacks as well; it was even heavier, so they had to rest after walking for a while. Wei Lanxiang and the others were used to mountain picking and did not think it was too difficult, but Lu Gu felt the weight more keenly.

When he was at Lu’s house, he would follow Lu Daxiang and the others to gather chestnuts, but after bringing them back, he didn’t eat much. He only ate them on the hillside when Du Hehua sent him out to look for them; everyone in the village went chestnut-picking, and he could find only a few, but at least chestnuts didn’t need roasting or boiling—they could be peeled and eaten. He still liked autumn very much; when chestnuts and wild fruits ripened, they could satisfy his hunger, so the weight on his back wouldn’t cling to him so much. So even though the bamboo basket was heavy, he was happy—the more he found, the more he could eat.

The chestnut grove lay far from the courtyard. They set off in the morning and returned by early afternoon. They wouldn’t have dared to roam the hills too far, lest they not return by nightfall, so they limited themselves to chestnuts today.

“It’s a good day this year. There are so many wild chestnuts. Let’s rest for today. Tomorrow we’ll go pick the hazelnuts. We’ll pull the cart over and push them back together, or it’ll be too heavy with only bamboo baskets,” Wei Lanxiang said, rubbing her shoulders.

“That’s right. It’s better to bring the cart tomorrow,” Ji Qiuyue agreed. They hadn’t gathered this much in the last year or two; they used to be full with bamboo baskets and a sack, though the year before was about the same as this year.

After Lu Gu set down the bamboo basket, he fetched water to wash everyone’s dirty hands. After resting and eating, seeing Shen Yan walking slowly, he asked whether she would go with them to pick wild soapberries. After washing his hands moments before, there were only three left in the small basket; not far from the yard stood a big wild soapberry tree, from which he picked the ones he used to sell in town.

They planned to go out to pick wild soapberries. Shen Xuanqing took the dogs and followed, leaving Wei Lanxiang and Ji Qiuyue resting in the room. After all, Shen Yan was young, and her legs weren’t tired after her recovery; picking wild soapberries would be fun for her, and she was naturally happy.

The sun was heading west, but it was not yet evening. About a quarter of an hour later, they arrived beneath the wild soapberry tree. Lu Gu, even Shen Yan, went to pick the wild soapberries. They picked the larger ones. Shen Xuanqing didn’t climb the tree; he stood beneath it and helped by using a basket to catch the falling berries. He was tall, so lifting his arm was convenient. Lu Gu could pick them with Shen Yan and toss them into the basket.

The wild soapberries were round, like fruit, but not red. They stayed green from flowering to dropping. The ones that fell to the ground were too ripe and left a white froth that could cleanse hands and hair, so no one picked them from the ground—only from the tree. The tree was large, and the berries it produced were also big, only smaller than quail eggs, and one harvest could last two days, which is why Lu Gu had sold them well before.

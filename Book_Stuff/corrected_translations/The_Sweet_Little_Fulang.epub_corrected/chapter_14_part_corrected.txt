Chapter 14


Rich people didn’t care if they paid ten taels of silver for a fresh wild kill. The point was to show it was rare. Besides, a young deer was delicious—succulent, tender, and nourishing. With silver, you could have it.

On the way here, Shen Xuanqing thought to himself: if the price got pressed down too hard, as long as it didn’t dip below five taels, it would at least cover half of the debt he owed.

The middle-aged man was greedy, but not reckless. He dreamed of a two-tael profit on top of ten taels, yet he didn’t dare to push it to sixteen taels for eight. This little errand was a big deal.

In the rice shop, Shen Xuanqing borrowed the shop’s scales to weigh the scattered silver the middle-aged man had brought. The rice shop wasn’t far from the stall where he and Lu Gu sold, and Lu Gu was keeping watch there alone at the moment.

He thanked the shopkeeper and was about to leave when the shopkeeper stopped him and asked how the rabbit had been sold. Since Shen Xuanqing had borrowed someone else’s scales, he offered a modest price. He’d earned a lot today; a little profit was nothing.

Since Shen Xuanqing had entered the rice shop, Lu Gu had been glancing toward it from time to time, and now he breathed a little easier to see him come out.

“The shopkeeper wants to check,” Shen Xuanqing explained, “it’s almost time to go back.” He picked up the bamboo basket and the items on the ground as soon as he approached.

Lu Gu followed him into the rice shop.

In the end, the two small rabbits brought in fifty copper coins. On ordinary days they could fetch seventy or eighty copper coins, but these were wild game—caught in the mountains, not common chickens and ducks.

Seeing that he had sold them so cheaply, the rice shopkeeper’s friendly smile widened a little. He said if Shen Xuanqing brought any other wild game in the future, he could come by; even if he didn’t buy, he could help by pointing him to other shopkeepers. If he needed to buy rice, it would be cheaper for him to come there.

Shen Xuanqing knew how to keep things smooth in the moment. They were all customers to each other, and their familiarity wasn’t a disadvantage, but a kind of extra friendship. After a few words of polite hospitality, he left with Lu Gu.

Eight taels of silver nestled in his bosom, and Shen Xuanqing let out a long, private sigh of relief. Only then did he truly feel at ease, and a wave of joy rose up at once. It was the first time he’d grown big enough to call this kind of luck his own; even the rest of his family had never earned so much money at once.

Yesterday, when he had hauled a young deer down from the mountains, he’d known luck had smiled on him, but he hadn’t yet received the money.

When the money wasn’t in his hands, he seldom showed it. Not only was he tight-lipped, but he wasn’t even in a good mood. This stemmed from something that happened when he was eleven years old.

For ordinary people, when they found something valuable and could sell it, they would be overjoyed. Even if they knew the wealth shouldn’t be announced broadly, they would still be secretly excited. Shen Xuanqing used to be the same.

When he was eleven, his stature and physique were not as good as they are now. He’d learned some hunting and a bit of boxing from the old hunter for a year or so. The bow and arrow had just shot its first prey.

That year, it was his first time hitting prey with bow and arrow. He was naturally excited and ran to his master with the rabbit. The old hunter wasn’t a stern old man, but a kind, rosy-cheeked elder. Seeing him hit the target, the hunter praised him as great.

The hunter hunted something and wanted to sell it for money. After taking it home and showing it to his family, Shen Xuanqing decided to go to town to sell the rabbit, counting it as the beginning of his career as a hunter.

His family said he could fetch at least thirty copper coins, and he believed so too. But when he arrived in town, the rabbit buyer saw that he was just a kid, and offered only twenty copper coins.

He didn’t want to sell, and was about to leave, but the rogue merchant clutched the rabbit and forced a sale—almost leading to a fight. A few peddlers couldn’t stand it and stepped forward to help, shouting that this wasn’t bullying.

The rogue was admonished by everyone, and in the end he was forced to pay five copper coins more.

Shen Xuanqing had thought he could fetch thirty copper coins, but he finally came home with twenty-five, sulking and downcast. When his family asked, although they were relieved, they said he showed real promise: if he could sell these things properly, the scoundrels would not simply bully the weak.

Because he’d been forced to endure such bullying, Wei Lanxiang specially steamed egg custard to comfort him, and secretly tucked ten copper coins into his hand to buy preserved fruit and snacks.

Fenggu Town was large, and they didn’t know that rogue. Even if they wanted to call back to demand an explanation or vent their anger, there was nowhere to look.

Shen Xuanqing eventually let the matter go, but the memory of that “tragedy”—the highs and lows of that day—stayed with him. Since then, when he set out in the morning, he never told his family how much the deer could fetch.

In fact, two years earlier he and that rogue had met near a tofu shop. He had been passing by and recognized the man with the big mole on his face. When he saw the rogue swindle a seven-year-old child, he shook his head in disappointment, realizing that the rogue was nothing but a bully who preyed on the weak.

The boy had been playing by the river and had caught two big fish. He tied them with a straw rope and was taking them home. The rogue stopped him and said he wanted to buy them. Hearing that he could turn them into money, the child’s eyes widened. He agreed, but the rogue offered only three copper coins.

That price wasn’t enough to attract a seven-year-old, so the boy frowned and walked away. Seeing he couldn’t swindle him, the rogue grabbed the fish away.

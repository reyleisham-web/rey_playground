After a full day in the fields, Shen Xuanqing—the sturdy laborer—had worked himself nearly to exhaustion. Soon he fell into a deep sleep. Lu Gu, lying on the inner side, gently patted the fretful Lingjun until the child settled into dreams as well.

But in the middle of the night, as soon as the child cried, Shen Xuanqing’s eyes snapped open. Lu Gu woke too.

“Wet again,” Lu Gu said after checking Lingjun. Moonlight spilled through the window paper tonight, bright as day. He pulled the wet diaper from Lingjun.

Shen Xuanqing grabbed a clean one from the head of the bed, his voice hoarse with sleep but clear. “I’ll change it. No need for you to get up.”

As he spoke, he got to work. Lu Gu had no choice but to lie back down, thinking that tomorrow night he’d sleep on the outer side so Shen Xuanqing wouldn’t have to rise again.

Fortunately, Lingjun was easy to soothe. Once changed into a dry diaper, no longer uncomfortable, he stopped crying after a few gentle rocks in his father’s arms—not tormenting his sleepy dad any longer.

Children were like that, unbound by time. They cried if they wet or dirtied their diapers at night, or if they were hungry, all without a fixed schedule. Tonight it was a wet diaper, not hunger. Earlier in the night, Shen Xuanqing had even had to feed him milk fruit.

There was no fire in the stove or in the earthen stove at night. On moonless nights, they had to ask others to strike a light. For this, Shen Xuanqing had even spent money on a fire striker from town; with a single puff, sparks would flare. It was convenient, though the fire striker was a bit pricey—even the cheapest cost sixty copper coins. (fire striker, huǒ zhézi: a traditional tinder pouch or portable flint-and-steel fire-starting kit, historically made of treated paper or hemp, blown on to ignite embers.)

After putting the child down, Lu Gu instinctively patted Lingjun a few times and murmured, “Sleep now.” Shen Xuanqing mumbled an “Mm” in his drowsiness before sinking back into silence, utterly spent.

Meanwhile, the ewe had given birth to a lamb right in the midst of the firewood bean and peanut harvest. Shen Yaoqing was called back by Ji Qiuyue and stood by attentively. Fortunately, the weather wasn’t cold lately, and the black-hoofed ewe wasn’t a first-time mother; the birth went smoothly without complications.

Xuanqing had rushed back from the fields in a fluster, but all that turned to joy once the lamb was born. This was their family’s first lambing. Beside him, Ji Qiuyue held Zhao’er, her face bright with happiness. If they kept breeding more, they could eventually have a whole flock to herd. Although these black-hoofed sheep hadn’t been raised long and retained a bit of wildness from the mountains, making them hard to pasture.

In the evening, when Wei Lanxiang, Shen Yan, and Shen Xuanqing returned, they, too, were delighted to see the lamb.

In the dog days of summer, the family had taken last year’s grown lamb to town to slaughter and sell, leaving only the ram and two ewes. Perhaps because the first ewe had been with the ram longer, she had conceived; the later ewe showed no signs of pregnancy. Shen Xuanqing and Shen Yaoqing once joked that the ram might be picky, perhaps because it had spent so long with the first ewe, and refused to go near the second. They didn’t force it. Next year, they’d keep more sheep together so they could all breed then.

After the harvest of autumn firewood beans and peanuts, there was still daily work—drying and threshing—but no complaints. By month’s end, little Lingjun was a full month old, and Lu Gu could finally go outside.

Lingjun was born, and the lamb was thriving—one happy event after another. Wei Lanxiang had been in high spirits lately, cheerful to everyone she met.

On the day of the full-month celebration, Shen Xuanqing was overjoyed. Since Lu Gu had no maternal family, he had long prepared a silver longevity lock for the child, as impressive as the others’. The feast for relatives and friends was lavish, with all kinds of fruit, pastries, wine, and meat, making for a lively day.

Since coming to the Shen family, Lu Gu had eaten well and slept soundly, his health gradually improving. After giving birth, he suffered no complications, having rested fully for thirty days to recover. The day after the celebration, while Lingjun slept, he and Ji Qiuyue strolled by the village river to unwind.

On their way back to the new house, they ran into Granny A-Jin and stopped to chat for a bit.

After they left, Granny A-Jin watched Lu Gu’s retreating figure with envy. She had lived in Qingxi Village for years, and few people could afford to hire help during confinement. She used to think Ji Qiuyue was lucky not having to work during pregnancy or after birth. Lu Gu’s situation seemed even more blessed. Not even the most gossipy villagers could help but voice some envy when talking about him.

The moment they stepped into the courtyard, Guaizi pounced over. Lu Gu smiled and rubbed his head. He had only heard Shen Xuanqing talk about the lamb, and today he could finally see it for himself. Not to mention the chickens and ducks, he hadn’t seen the calf in ages and the pigs in the pen; all of them had grown and fattened considerably over the past month.

After summer, the heat had diminished, and the stench from the manure pile had faded. The threshing ground was spread with firewood bean and peanut stalks. A few more days of drying, and they could be gathered.

Ji Qiuyue went straight to the backyard to work. Zhao’er was at home with Wei Lanxiang—already a year old, he could gnaw on steamed buns by himself. With milk fruit at home, she didn’t have to worry much if he got hungry. When she had given birth to Zhao’er, her milk had been plentiful, so she hadn’t asked Shen Yaoqing to gather milk fruit; she’d left some for other families’ children.

Lu Gu went to the sheep pen first to see the lamb, Guaizi wagging his tail and following closely.

“Growing so well,” he said from outside the pen. The lamb let out a soft “baa,” making him chuckle. “Are you talking to me?”

The lamb’s “baa” drew a silly grin. Then, from the ox pen, a “moo” sounded—the calf had recognized him and was calling.

Lu Gu’s heart swelled with joy. After a month apart, the calf still remembered him. He went over to refill its water trough. The calf didn’t drink right away; it nudged his hand with its big head before lowering its muzzle to gulp down the water.

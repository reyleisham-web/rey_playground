Chapter 39

Qingxi

The Qingxi River flowed out of the mountains, moistening the earth wherever it went, and stretched toward unknown distances. In the broad stretch of river ahead, boats rocked gently. From afar they looked small, like the flat boats the locals spoke of. Both people and poultry needed boats to cross the river.

In the narrow section of the stream there stood a bridge—the Liangzhang Bridge. Lu Gu crossed the bridge with Shen Xuanqing, who carried a small bamboo basket on his back. Niujia Village lay on the far shore of the Qingxi River, distant. It took almost thirty-five minutes to walk along the riverbank to Liangzhang Bridge. Most of the villages and towns here were built along the river. From the bridge, forks in the road led toward the southwest avenue; Fenggu Town lay ahead, but after crossing, Shen Xuanqing had said that Niujia Village lay beyond one more village.

The bridge thronged with people going to and fro, peddlers shouting as they went, selling needles, thread, knives, groceries, fruit, cakes, and biscuits. A woman with a basket led a child, who stretched his hand to grab candied hawthorns, and the woman hurried him down the bridge by the arm.

A sedan chair was carried onto the bridge from the opposite side, its window curtain pushed aside, and the fulang sitting inside looked out. A gray-haired but elegant old lady rode a donkey, led by a half-grown boy at the front, the donkey’s hooves stamping softly as they approached her home.

Liangzhang Bridge mirrored the town’s bustle. The Liangzhang market opened on the first and fifteenth day of the Lunar New Year, drawing people from all corners to buy and sell. Ships on both sides of the bridge unloaded their wares. Shen Xuanqing had once carried large bales here and unloaded timber, so he was well acquainted with the surroundings.

The candied-haw hawker passed by with a load of wood on his shoulders. Lu Gu hadn’t been to Fenggu Town many times, much less this particular place; he watched curiously as Shen Xuanqing called out to stop the candied-haw seller.

Seeing another customer approach, the peddler grinned and asked politely, “A string is three copper coins. How many strings would you like?”

“One string is fine. Pick a big one.” Shen Xuanqing drew a new purse embroidered with tiger patterns from his chest. Lu Gu had sewn it in his spare time; it had been given to Shen Xuanqing the night before, and today he was just putting it on.

“Okay.” As soon as the peddler collected the money, he took a piece from the pile. The pile was fairly uniform; otherwise it would be hard to sell if someone picked at it.

Shen Xuanqing tucked the purse under his arm, not intending to keep it for himself, so the peddler handed it to Lu Gu. There were eight bright red hawthorns, each wrapped in sugar syrup. These treats were common in the town’s streets and alleys, but country folk didn’t buy them easily—where would they get the spare money? Besides, hawthorn was a favorite with children, though some adults still disliked its sour tang.

The peddler moved on. Lu Gu looked up at Shen Xuanqing, hawthorns in hand, eyes filled with worry and caution. “Let’s eat,” Shen Xuanqing said.

Candied hawthorns were not precious, and Liangzhang Bridge was like a small fair. It wasn’t unusual to buy a snack for the fulang. When he was young, he had gone to fairs with his family, tasting delicious foods; at the big markets he bought what he loved most, and when he returned to the village, he shared it with his friends. For families whose circumstances were not bad, visiting the market to buy something they didn’t usually eat seemed a necessity.

Shen Xuanqing walked down the bridge, Lu Gu taking half a step behind him. Lu Gu hesitated before biting into the hawthorn at the top, its sugar syrup gleaming; the flesh of the fruit was sour. He tasted it, thinking it wasn’t good to eat alone, so he turned his head and whispered, “Do you want to eat?”

Before he finished speaking, the candied hawthorn was passed to Shen Xuanqing. “No, you eat,” Shen Xuanqing replied; he didn’t much like candied hawthorns himself.

After the two had stepped off the bridge, the bustle continued. A fortune-teller with a braided beard twisted as he explained his hexagrams, and the air was thick with the fragrance of all sorts of snacks. People sat in taverns and teahouses. When Shen Xuanqing saw a pancake seller, he paused and bought two pancakes, which cost six copper coins.

The hot pancake was wrapped in a small piece of oily paper and could be eaten in hand. It wasn’t very large, but the grease made it crisp and soft. Shen Xuanqing gave one to Lu Gu; the other he ate in three quick bites. With both hands full of food, Lu Gu didn’t know which to eat first. After a moment’s decision, he finished the pancake first, for fear it would get cold.

He found himself watching Shen Xuanqing as he ate. Even when his mother was around, she never bought him two delicious foods at once, filling both hands. A tremor of joy rose in him, brightening his eyes as he looked at Shen Xuanqing, but gradually a strange sourness seeped into his heart and eyes. He never imagined someone would treat him so well, even more generously than his mother did.

When Shen Xuanqing turned his head, he saw his little fulang’s eyes were wet, showing a trace of pity. He was surprised, not understanding what had happened or why he was crying, and he asked, “What’s wrong? Why are you crying?”

Lu Gu shook his head instinctively, signaling that it was nothing. After thinking for a moment, he whispered, “It’s a little sour.”

Shen Xuanqing looked at the hawthorn in his hand and felt relieved. Hawthorn could bring tears to people, he warned, “Don’t eat it if it’s too sour.”

Lu Gu’s sourness came from within his heart, but he didn’t know how to express it. He felt embarrassed to speak, afraid of being scolded as a mourning ghost. Moreover, those feelings would be broken the moment he spoke. He was too embarrassed to explain, so he said quickly, “It’s not sour—there’s only one left.”

Shen Xuanqing thought it a pity for him but couldn’t take it, and he said nothing more. A warmth blossomed in Lu Gu’s chest—perhaps a ger-like warmth, he realized, though he didn’t know what to call it.
